My ambition is to design the "algorithms for settlement" for a new era. At the School for Design & Science (SDS), I will explore how resilient community models—a concept I call _Kaisoka_—can emerge from the bottom up in an age of population decline. Rather than relying on top-down urban planning, I am determined to build computational models where spatial design itself becomes the medium for fostering collaboration and collective intelligence.

My journey to this question began three years ago with the "Valley of the Wind" project at Keio University's Ataka Lab, a place dedicated to exploring every possibility for humans to thrive in low-density spaces. As a sophomore, I was convinced that purely digital technologies like DAOs and sophisticated voting systems held the key to creating self-organizing communities, and I immersed myself in this research.

Soon, however, I hit an unavoidable real-world wall that digital rules alone could not overcome. I was struck by two acute realizations. First, human collaboration is deeply rooted in the gritty, tangible details of the physical environment: road layouts, the use of public space, the physical distance between neighbors. Second, the value generated from these interactions is far too dynamic and diverse to be captured or evaluated by any fixed system. This new understanding caused a decisive shift in my focus: from designing rules in cyberspace to exploring the "generative rules" embedded in the real world itself. My work on chatbot development and settlement simulations became a concrete process of trial and error, attempts to decipher this complex interplay between the digital and the physical.

To deepen my understanding of emergent phenomena, I proactively approached Professor Takashi Ikegami's lab at the University of Tokyo, gaining the opportunity to participate in their daily discussions. The dialogue there taught me that a generative approach of "building before analyzing," along with concepts like embodiment, are indispensable to my research. Above all, I became certain that this intellectual pursuit was, at its core, profoundly enjoyable.

This intellectual journey—from an overconfidence in the digital to an appreciation of the hybrid nature of space—has shaped who I am today. I have learned the craft of defining my own questions and building the systems to explore them. The "antidisciplinary" inquiry and project-based practice that SDS champions are no longer just an attractive environment for me; they are an essential requirement for my research to succeed. I am confident that at SDS, I can contribute to laying the groundwork for the next generation of living spaces.
